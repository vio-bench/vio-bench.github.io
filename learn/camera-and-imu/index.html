2.1 Camera Images and Projection

A camera records an array of pixel intensities or colors at each image timestamp. Feature detection and tracking extract two-dimensional point coordinates from these images. A projection model relates those coordinates to the three-dimensional geometry viewed by the camera. We use point coordinates as a representative observation; direct methods can instead compare image intensities.

Let Cpf=(X,Y,Z)⊤{}^{C}\mathbf{p}_f=(X,Y,Z)^\topCpf​=(X,Y,Z)⊤ denote an environment point expressed in the camera frame. We take the camera's optical axis as +ZC+Z_C+ZC​, with XCX_CXC​ pointing right and YCY_CYC​ down in the image. For an ideal pinhole camera with known intrinsics, zero skew, and no lens distortion, its observed pixel coordinates zf=(u,v)⊤\mathbf{z}_f=(u,v)^\topzf​=(u,v)⊤ follow:

The focal lengths fx,fyf_x,f_yfx​,fy​ and principal-point coordinates cx,cyc_x,c_ycx​,cy​ are expressed in pixels. The principal point is where the optical axis meets the ideal image plane. The point coordinates X,Y,ZX,Y,ZX,Y,Z use a common length unit; in VIO we express them in meters. Here ZZZ is depth along the optical axis. Projection divides by depth. Multiplying all three point coordinates by the same positive factor leaves their ratios unchanged:

Thus, under this calibrated model, a single point observation determines a viewing ray, but does not determine the point's distance along it. The camera pose determines a fixed environment point's coordinates in {C}\{C\}{C} and therefore its predicted projection. Observations of fixed points across images provide constraints on camera motion and scene geometry. The transform between camera and IMU coordinates is described in Notation, Section 1.2.

2.2 IMU Measurements

An IMU provides timestamped, three-axis gyroscope and accelerometer readings, rather than direct orientation, velocity, or position measurements. We use an additive model with scale and axis errors assumed corrected or negligible. Readings are expressed along the common IMU axes. We neglect Earth rotation, treat {G}\{G\}{G} as locally inertial, and assume constant gravity.

The gyroscope measures angular velocity, which describes how orientation changes over time. Its measurement model is

ωm=Iω+bg+ng.\boldsymbol{\omega}_m={}^{I}\boldsymbol{\omega}+\mathbf{b}_g+\mathbf{n}_g.ωm​=Iω+bg​+ng​.

Here Iω{}^{I}\boldsymbol{\omega}Iω is the IMU's angular velocity relative to {G}\{G\}{G}, expressed along the IMU axes. Its direction gives the instantaneous rotation axis under the right-hand rule, and its magnitude gives angular speed; neither specifies the current orientation. The vectors bg\mathbf{b}_gbg​ and ng\mathbf{n}_gng​ are bias and measurement noise. All terms have units of radians per second. For the global-to-IMU rotation GIR{}^{I}_{G}\mathbf{R}GI​R,

GIR˙=−⌊Iω⌋ GIR.{}^{I}_{G}\dot{\mathbf R}=-\lfloor{}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega\rfloor\,{}^{I}_{G}\mathbf R.GI​R˙=−⌊Iω⌋GI​R.

A dot denotes a time derivative. The skew-symmetric operator satisfies ⌊u⌋v=u×v\lfloor\mathbf u\rfloor\mathbf v=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v⌊u⌋v=u×v; the floor-shaped brackets do not mean rounding. The minus sign follows the global-to-IMU convention. Integration requires an initial orientation relative to {G}\{G\}{G}; the gyroscope does not supply it. Integrating three components independently does not generally give roll, pitch, and yaw.

Bias and noise play different roles. We model bias as an unknown offset that may change slowly, and noise as zero-mean random measurement error. An estimator subtracts an estimated bias before integration; the true bias and individual noise samples remain unknown. Bias errors and noise both affect the integrated state. Zero-mean noise need not cancel over a finite interval.

The accelerometer measures specific force, the non-gravitational force per unit mass acting on the sensor. Its measurement model is

am=GIR(GaI−Gg)+ba+na.\mathbf{a}_m={}^{I}_{G}\mathbf{R}\left({}^{G}\mathbf{a}_I-{}^{G}\mathbf{g}\right)+\mathbf{b}_a+\mathbf{n}_a.am​=GI​R(GaI​−Gg)+ba​+na​.

Here GaI=d(GvI)/dt{}^{G}\mathbf{a}_I=d({}^{G}\mathbf{v}_I)/dtGaI​=d(GvI​)/dt is the IMU origin's motion acceleration and Gg{}^{G}\mathbf{g}Gg is gravitational acceleration, both expressed in {G}\{G\}{G}. The reading am\mathbf{a}_mam​, bias ba\mathbf{b}_aba​, and noise na\mathbf{n}_ana​ use IMU axes and have units of meters per second squared, as do the global acceleration terms. The same bias/noise distinction applies to both sensors. IMU measurement model.

Rotating the bias-corrected reading into {G}\{G\}{G} with (GIR)⊤({}^{I}_{G}\mathbf{R})^\top(GI​R)⊤ and adding gravity gives an acceleration estimate. An incorrect tilt can produce apparent motion acceleration even at rest. Velocity and position then require time integration and their initial values.

Its accelerometer reads (0,0,g)⊤(0,0,g)^\top(0,0,g)⊤: the table supports it against gravity. In ideal, non-rotating free fall it reads zero, even though the sensor accelerates under gravity.
