4.1 What Must Be Initialized

An IMU reports angular velocity and specific force. Integrating them requires an initial orientation, velocity, position, and bias estimates. VIO initialization obtains a state from which inertial propagation and visual constraints can be combined. If monocular images first provide an up-to-scale reconstruction, its metric scale must also be determined. Calibrated stereo observations can constrain metric depth, but do not directly provide initial velocity or IMU biases. VIO initialization.

Some initial values define the reference frame rather than physical information recovered from measurements. For ordinary VIO with fixed calibration, informative motion, and no absolute reference, global translation and heading about gravity remain unobservable. We may choose an initial position and heading to define the global frame. This choice is different from estimating tilt relative to gravity, velocity, or biases; setting those unknowns to zero does not make them correct.

The initial quantities interact. A tilt error rotates the measured specific force into the wrong global direction. At rest, the rotated specific force and the added gravity vector then fail to cancel. With correct biases and a pure 1∘1^{\circ}1∘ tilt error, the horizontal acceleration error is 9.81sin⁡(1∘)≈0.17 m/s29.81\sin(1^{\circ})\approx0.17\,\mathrm{m/s^2}9.81sin(1∘)≈0.17m/s2. Incorrect bias or velocity also changes the subsequently integrated motion. Visual updates may correct some errors, but successful later correction should not be assumed.

Initialization should therefore provide both initial estimates and an appropriate description of their uncertainty or prior strength. In a filter, an unjustifiably small initial covariance can give excessive weight to a poor state. In an optimizer, an overly strong prior has a similar effect. The initial state need not be exact, but it must be compatible with the measurements and with the assumptions of the estimator that follows.

4.2 Starting at Rest

A stationary start supplies additional assumptions: zero angular velocity and zero linear velocity. Under Section 2.2's calibrated model, neglecting Earth rotation and assuming approximately constant biases during averaging,

An overbar denotes a mean over the stationary interval. The gyro mean can estimate bias. The accelerometer mean contains both gravity-related specific force and bias; one stationary orientation does not separate them. If accelerometer bias is already corrected, negligible, or constrained by a justified prior, gravity gives the initial tilt. It does not determine heading about gravity.

Stationarity must be checked rather than inferred from low IMU variance alone. Straight motion at constant velocity and fixed orientation can have the same ideal IMU readings as rest. Image observations or other information can help distinguish these cases. Applying zero velocity while the platform is actually moving supplies an incorrect constraint.

A stationary initializer can set initial velocity to zero only while that condition is supported. Choosing initial position and heading defines the reference frame as in Section 4.1. These operations do not provide monocular scene depth: a single image still gives viewing rays. Subsequent visual tracking and motion are needed to establish the scene constraints used by the estimator.

Average readings over the verified stationary interval using the corrected IMU axes and units. Averaging can reduce independent zero-mean measurement noise, but it does not remove an unknown bias. Supply a justified accelerometer-bias estimate or prior, and retain uncertainty in the resulting tilt and bias values.

When the platform starts moving, stop imposing a stationary velocity constraint unless stationarity is detected again. Record the selected startup interval and the evidence used to accept it. A known stationary start is useful information; a falsely assumed stationary start can initialize the estimator with an incorrect state.

4.3 Starting in Motion

Dynamic initialization uses an interval of motion. A common approach reconstructs camera motion and scene structure from images, then relates this motion to integrated IMU measurements to estimate scale, gravity direction, velocities, and biases. Joint approaches instead solve coupled visual and inertial unknowns together. These are different ways to obtain starting estimates; they do not make every motion informative. The accelerometer still measures gravity-related specific force, so these terms can be nonzero at rest and cancel the explicit gravity contributions.

Across multiple intervals, the same scale, gravity vector, and consistent bias assumptions must explain both streams. With noisy data, the integrated terms are uncertain, bias-dependent quantities in a weighted fit. One interval does not determine all unknowns, and every initializer need not estimate all biases simultaneously.

A refinement step can then improve the initial state by explaining both measurement streams with the same motion and calibrated sensor relation. Bias assumptions differ between methods: some estimate both biases at startup, while others constrain or defer weakly determined quantities. State which quantities were estimated, fixed, or initialized from a prior. A dynamic start should not be processed using a stationary-start assumption merely because it is easier.

More samples are useful only when they constrain the unknowns. Observability asks whether different states can produce the same ideal measurements. Even when a state is observable in principle, a short or weakly informative interval can make its numerical estimate poorly determined. The conditions depend on the sensor configuration, motion, scene geometry, and parameters being estimated.

For monocular images, translational parallax helps determine scene structure. Image rotation without translational parallax does not determine feature depth from the images alone. Distant features and small translations can also provide weak depth information. The IMU contributes additional motion constraints; the information in the image stream and the inertial stream must be considered together.

As a simple scale-degenerate case, consider an ideal monocular rig traveling in a straight line at constant velocity and fixed orientation, with no known scene distances. Scaling the scene and the traveled distances preserves the images. The motion acceleration is zero, so the accelerometer does not distinguish those scales. Repeating that motion for longer does not supply the missing metric information. This example assumes the stated motion and sensor model; it is not a claim that all nearly straight trajectories are unusable.

Changing acceleration and rotation can help separate scale, gravity, and bias effects, but large motion alone is not a guarantee. Useful initialization also requires trackable observations, correct calibration, and reliable timestamps. An initialization method can inspect residuals, sensitivity, or uncertainty before accepting a state, and wait for additional information when needed. The required motion and acceptance criteria should follow the method's model rather than an arbitrary fixed waiting time. Inertial initialization.

4.4 Checking Initialization and Recovering

A finite pose output is not sufficient evidence that initialization is reliable. Check whether the accepted state explains both image and inertial observations, whether estimated biases and velocities are physically plausible, and whether any estimated monocular scale is positive and well constrained. Numerical convergence says that a stopping condition was reached; it does not by itself establish that the state is correct.

These checks must match the quantities actually estimated. If gravity magnitude is fixed by the model, reproducing that value is not an independent validation of initialization. If it is estimated, compare it with a justified local reference or physical range. Bias bounds must match the sensor and units. For monocular images with unknown scene scale, small reprojection residuals do not by themselves validate metric scale. Use several complementary checks and retain uncertainty in weakly constrained quantities.

After startup, initialization is followed by ongoing estimation. New observations can refine the state, while tracking losses or inconsistent measurements may require rejection, recovery, or reinitialization. A restart can define a new local frame; its poses cannot be treated as continuous with the previous trajectory unless their relationship is established. Reinitialization restores a starting state, not estimates for the preceding unobserved interval.

Record the time of the first accepted estimate, rejected initialization attempts, resets, and the identity of each output segment. Keep initialization delay separate from processing delay after startup. This makes it possible to distinguish late startup, interrupted tracking, and a continuously available trajectory without guessing from a nonempty output file.

For a repeatable test, run startup from several points in a recording under the same documented settings. Check both acceptance time and estimation quality after acceptance. A method that initializes in one favorable interval may behave differently under another motion or visual condition. Initialization should be tested as part of the estimation process, including how the system detects and handles an unreliable start.
