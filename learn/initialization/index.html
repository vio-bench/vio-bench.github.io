Understand what initialization must recover

Explain why starting a VIO estimator requires more than the first image and how to diagnose an initialization failure.

The state must start somewhere

Inertial propagation needs an initial orientation, velocity, position, and bias estimate. Monocular visual reconstruction may also need its scale connected to inertial measurements. In ordinary VIO without external global references, global translation and rotation about gravity remain unobservable. Choosing a starting position and yaw fixes a local coordinate convention; gravity direction, velocity, biases, and scale depend on measurements and assumptions.

The initial uncertainty matters as well. If an uncertain quantity is initialized with excessive confidence, later observations may be unable to correct it appropriately. An initializer therefore supplies both an estimate and, in many formulations, uncertainty information for the estimator that follows.

A stationary start is an assumption

A stationary interval can support gravity alignment and gyroscope bias estimation. A single static orientation cannot independently identify arbitrary accelerometer bias and tilt; the initializer needs assumptions about them. OpenVINS uses a previous stationary measurement window and can wait for a subsequent acceleration change before starting. The sensor must actually satisfy the assumed stationary condition.

Low variation alone is not proof of zero motion: constant acceleration can also produce nearly constant accelerometer readings. Gravity constrains roll and pitch under the stationary assumption; it does not independently determine heading about the gravity direction.

Dynamic initialization uses joint constraints

A dynamic initializer combines image tracks and inertial measurements over a time window. OpenVINS documents a process using inertial preintegration, a constrained initial solve, and subsequent maximum-likelihood refinement with uncertainty recovery. The purpose is to obtain mutually consistent initial motion and sensor states.

Motion is useful only when it contributes information. An image sequence with little usable parallax, interrupted tracks, or inconsistent timestamps can prevent a well-conditioned start. Moving faster is not a universal remedy because it can also produce blur and lost tracks.

Diagnose the stage that failed

First verify that both sensor streams reach the estimator with the intended timestamps and calibration. Then inspect the startup window, feature tracks, initializer status, and first published state. Record the time at which initialization succeeds. An estimator that never starts and one that loses tracking halfway through a sequence have different failure modes.

Keep the original recording intact when testing a new startup interval. Record the selected start time and configuration, and change one assumption at a time so the next result has an interpretable cause.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Why can a gravity-aligned stationary IMU still have an arbitrary initial yaw?

Reveal the explanation

Rotating the heading around the gravity direction does not change the gravity vector observed by that stationary IMU. A heading reference or an arbitrary local convention is needed; gravity alone does not fix yaw.
